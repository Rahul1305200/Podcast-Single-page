Hotel rooms should be designed around guest recovery, not just appearance. Keith Dickey, Sarah Walker and Wouter Dekker explore how spaces affect the nervous system, why noise and HVAC decisions deserve wellness input, the limits of biometric data in hospitality, and the privacy questions that come with handling health data.

Recorded with Future of Hospitality · 14 October 2026 · 1 hr 4 min

What this episode covers

A guest spends forty minutes in the spa and eight hours in the room. The conversation starts there, and keeps returning to the decisions nobody reviews.

  • Why the guest room, not the spa, decides whether someone actually recovers.
  • How noise and HVAC choices reach the nervous system long before anyone notices them.
  • What biometric data can and cannot tell a hotel about a guest.
  • The privacy line that handling health data draws across hospitality.
  • Where wellness belongs in a design brief, and who has to sign it off.

Transcript

The full conversation, lightly edited for readability.

Keith DickeyThe thing we keep coming back to is that a guest spends maybe forty minutes in a spa and eight hours in a room. The room is doing the work, whether or not anyone designed it to.

Sarah WalkerAnd it is doing that work invisibly. Nobody checks out and says the air handling unit cycled too often. They say they slept badly.

Wouter DekkerWhich is the problem, because the people specifying that equipment are optimising for cost and capacity. Nobody in that conversation is asked what it sounds like at three in the morning.

Keith DickeySo the question becomes who owns recovery in the building. Right now it is nobody, and that is why it keeps falling off the brief.
